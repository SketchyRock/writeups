# RVASEC15 Conference Retrospective

**Event:** RVASEC15 (Roanoke, VA Security Conference)  
**Dates:** April 12-13, 2026  
**Location:** Roanoke, Virginia  
**Significance:** First security conference attendance  
**How We Got Here:** Jake Kouns (industry luminary, security leader) gave JMU CDC tickets directly  
**Personal Goals:** Compete in CTF, network, learn from industry professionals  
**CTF Performance:** B team, solved 8 challenges in ~1 hour, then deprioritized competition  
**Real Value:** Professional networking, industry insights, career perspective

## Executive Summary

RVASEC15 was supposed to be my CTF competition debut. I prepared extensively beforehand, researching challenges, studying techniques, mentally preparing for a multi-day grind. Then I got there.

First day: completely abandoned CTF prep to network instead. Second day: solved 8 challenges quickly, then stopped to attend talks and meet people.

By the end, I realized my initial plan was backwards. I went to RVASEC15 to compete. I should have gone to RVASEC15 to connect.

This shift — from "win the CTF" to "learn from the industry" — was the most important professional realization of my cybersecurity journey so far. And it happened by accident, because I put down the laptop and talked to strangers instead.

## The Setup: Why RVASEC15 Was Different

### Getting the Tickets

Jake Kouns — a security legend in the industry, formerly CISO at several major companies, now running security initiatives — personally gave JMU CDC tickets to attend RVASEC15. Not through a standard sponsor channel. Directly. He knew our club advisor and thought our club was worth supporting.

This wasn't just "free conference tickets." It was implicit credibility. People knew that if JMU was there, we'd been vouched for by someone serious.

**In hindsight**, that credential made the entire experience different. People were willing to talk to me because they already had (indirect) social proof.

### Pre-Conference Preparation (Misguided)

I spent 2 weeks preparing for the CTF:
- Studied common CTF challenge types
- Practiced on HackTheBox and TryHackMe
- Researched RVASEC's historical CTF challenges
- Made a mental "game plan" for time allocation

**The Plan:**
- Day 1: Quick orientation, then *immediately* start CTF
- Day 2: Full-day CTF grind
- Evening: brief networking if time permits
- Leave with: trophy/placement + some business cards

**What Actually Happened:**
- Day 1: Got overwhelmed by number of interesting talks, met people in casual conversation, realized I was genuinely interested in what people do
- Day 2: Solved CTF challenges, but kept stopping to attend talks
- Left with: handful of deep conversations, business cards from people I'll actually remember, no trophy but something more valuable

## Day 1: The Networking Accident

### The Schedule I Ignored

RVASEC15 had a packed agenda:
- Morning: Keynotes on security trends
- Afternoon: Technical talks (malware analysis, cloud security, red teaming, threat intelligence)
- Evening: Networking mixer, sponsor booths

I arrived with my laptop, ready to start CTF challenges.

Instead, I went to the first keynote. Just to "learn the lay of the land."

### Why I Stopped Competing

**The Keynote:** A panel of security leaders (CISOs from Fortune 500 companies, security startup founders, government cybersecurity folks) discussing "The Future of Cybersecurity." 

One of them said something like: "The best security person isn't the one who knows the most tools. It's the person who can communicate what they know to non-technical stakeholders."

I realized: I've spent months grinding CTFs and competitions, trying to solve challenges faster. But I've never talked to someone outside a team environment about what I do.

**The Realization:** If I only ever grind on laptops, I'm training for a world that doesn't exist. Real security work is 30% technical, 70% communication + relationships + understanding business context.

So I closed my laptop and went to the speaker lounge.

### Conversations That Changed My Perspective

**Conversation 1: Threat Intelligence Lead at a Fortune 500 Company**

Me: "How do you get into threat intelligence?"

Her: "Honestly? You solve some puzzles to prove you can think, then you learn the job. But the learning part takes years and requires building relationships with other analysts."

Me: "Is it better to be really good at one thing or okay at many things?"

Her: "In my experience, deep specialization gets you the job. But once you're hired, your ability to *talk* about what you found determines if anyone listens. I've seen brilliant analysts get sidelined because they couldn't communicate findings to executives."

**This validated what I was learning at JMU's mock competition: specialization matters. But also validated that I need soft skills.**

**Conversation 2: Security Startup Founder**

Me: "How do you hire engineers at your startup?"

Him: "Looking for three things: technical skills, ability to learn fast, and *likeability*. Can I work with this person for 8 hours a day? Do they ask good questions or do they sulk when something is hard?"

Me: "Does the technical skill matter most?"

Him: "Not at our stage. We can teach technical stuff. We can't teach people to be pleasant collaborators."

**This was humbling. In competitions, you can be toxic and still win. In real jobs, personality matters.**

**Conversation 3: Red Team Operator (Penetration Tester)**

Me: "I'm trying to specialize in Windows privilege escalation. Is that marketable?"

Him: "Absolutely. But here's the thing — every red teamer can escalate privileges. What differentiates you is: can you document it clearly? Can you explain to a client why this vulnerability matters? Can you write a report they'll actually read?"

**Again: technical skills are table stakes. Soft skills are the differentiator.**

### The Networking Realization

By end of Day 1, I'd had 5+ conversations with professionals. Hadn't opened my CTF challenges once. Felt guilty about it for approximately 20 minutes, then realized: this is more valuable.

## Day 2: The 1-Hour CTF Attempt

### When I Actually Competed

Felt obligated to at least try the CTF. Showed up at 9 AM with laptop.

**The CTF Setup:**
- Jeopardy-style challenges (crypto, reversing, web, forensics, miscellaneous)
- Live network with hidden flags
- 8-hour competition (9 AM - 5 PM)

**My Performance:**
- 9:00-10:00 AM: Solved 8 challenges (mix of easy-medium difficulty)
- 10:00 AM: Attended a talk on "Red Teaming in Real Engagements"
- 10:45 AM: Back to CTF, tried one hard challenge, gave up after 15 minutes
- 11:00 AM: Decided the hard challenges would take hours, talked to organizers instead about their CTF design philosophy
- 11:30 AM - 5 PM: Attended talks, networked with sponsors, skipped CTF

### Why I Stopped

**The Honest Reason:** I was on the B team (secondary team), and at a conference with limited time, I had to choose: grind hard problems for possible 1-2 more flags, or learn from the people I flew here to meet.

I chose people.

**The B Team Context:** Our B team was competing "for learning," not for ranking. The main JMU team was back home, focused on serious preparation. For us, RVASEC was an opportunity to experience a real conference and learn from professionals. The CTF was secondary.

**The Trade-off:** Lost potential flags + placement. Gained conversations with security leaders, understanding of real industry problems, perspective on my career direction.

In hindsight: right choice.

### The CTF Performance (For Context)

8 challenges in 1 hour means I solved easy/medium tier:
- **Crypto:** Basic cipher (Caesar, XOR, Vigenere variant)
- **Reversing:** Simple binary with visible strings
- **Web:** SQLi and path traversal
- **Forensics:** File metadata extraction
- **Misc:** Steganography

By hour 2, challenges got hard (serious reversing, custom crypto, complex network exploitation). Knew I could eventually solve them, but it would take 4+ hours each.

Could've stayed glued to laptop and earned 2-3 more flags. Instead, I attended talks.

**In competitions, this is a bad choice. At a professional conference, it's the right choice.**

## The Talks I Attended Instead of Competing

### "Red Teaming in Real Engagements" (10:45 AM)

**Presenter:** Senior penetration tester with 10+ years experience

**Key Insights:**
- Real red teams don't use Metasploit (too obvious)
- Prefer custom tools and living-off-the-land techniques
- Social engineering is 70% of real red team success
- Most breaches start with phishing, not technical exploits

**Why This Mattered:** I've been focusing on technical exploitation (CVEs, privilege escalation, network exploitation). But real attacks are different. If I want to do offense, I need to understand attacker *incentives*, not just attacker *techniques*.

### "Threat Intelligence: From Raw Data to Decision" (1:00 PM)

**Presenter:** Threat intel analyst from a large tech company

**Key Insights:**
- Most threat intel work is *boring* (tracking indicators, pattern matching, correlation)
- The interesting part is: understanding geopolitical context to predict adversary behavior
- Needs: Python scripting, SQL, comfort with ambiguity, storytelling to executives

**Why This Mattered:** I'd assumed threat intel was pure technical work. Turns out it's mostly detective work + communication. If I specialized in threat intel, I'd need to be comfortable with incomplete information and good at explaining uncertainty.

### "Security in Startups vs. Enterprise" (2:30 PM)

**Presenter:** CTO of a mid-stage security startup, former CISO at a Fortune 500

**Key Insights:**
- Startups: you're doing security + engineering + ops (everything)
- Enterprise: you're doing security + politics + stakeholder management (everything)
- Neither is easier; just different tradeoffs
- Security career can start anywhere; the company matters less than the learning

**Why This Mattered:** I've been assuming I need to start at a big company. Turns out, startups can be equal or better for learning. Different risks, different rewards.

## The Booth Crawl (3:00-5:00 PM)

RVASEC had ~20 sponsor companies (security vendors, consulting firms, tech companies). Each had a booth with swag, demos, and recruiters.

**What I Expected:** Get free t-shirt, leave.

**What Actually Happened:** Had 5-10 minute conversations with recruiters from companies I'd actually like to work at someday.

Me: "Yeah, I've done CTFs but I'm focusing more on blue team now."

Recruiter: "That's actually valuable. A lot of red teamers don't understand defense, and a lot of defenders don't understand offense. You've got perspective."

Recruiter: "How much experience do you have with classified systems?"

Me: "None yet, just school."

### The Realization

By end of RVASEC, I had:
- 5 business cards from people who explicitly wanted to hear from me
- 3-4 email addresses for "reach out after graduation"
- One explicit job offer (pending graduation): "We want people like you"

I would NOT have gotten these from grinding the CTF for 8 hours. These came from conversations.

## The Biggest Learning: Competitions vs. Conferences

### What Competitions Teach

- Technical skills under pressure
- Problem-solving speed
- Resilience (keep trying when stuck)
- Specialization (go deep on one domain)

### What Conferences Teach

- Industry context (what problems actually matter)
- Career paths (multiple ways to "make it")
- Professional relationships (future colleagues, mentors, friends)
- Soft skills (communication, listening, curiosity)
- Personal brand (how you present yourself)

### The False Choice

I went to RVASEC thinking it was: competition *or* conference.

Actually, it's: competition *and* conference, with different benefits.

**The Insight:** I can grind CTFs anytime (they're online, free, unlimited). I can only attend RVASEC once a year. I should optimize for what's unique about being *there in person*.

That's relationships.

## Specific Career Insights from RVASEC

### 1. Specialization is Real, But Optionality Matters

Everyone I talked to said: "Get good at something specific."

But they also said: "Being good at one thing + understanding three other things is better than being good at one thing + knowing nothing else."

**Implication:** Keep Windows defense as primary specialization. But continue learning offense + networking + threat intel at a moderate level. Don't ignore other domains.

### 2. Soft Skills are Underrated

This was mentioned directly or indirectly in every conversation.

"Can you write a report?"
"Can you explain this to a non-technical CEO?"
"Are you someone I'd want in a 6-hour meeting?"

**Implication:** I need to practice communication. Write-ups, blog posts, presentations, explaining technical things to non-technical people. This is as important as technical chops.

### 3. Government Work is More Accessible Than I Thought

Two different people mentioned security clearances and government work.

"We sponsor clearances. That's our investment in you."

**Implication:** Government/contractor work is a viable path, and clearance is valuable (takes 6+ months, but companies bear cost). Could be good stepping stone.

### 4. Security is Genuinely Interesting as a Career

This sounds obvious, but I mean it differently: everyone I talked to was *genuinely interested* in what they do. Not just for the paycheck.

They asked me thoughtful questions. They listened to my interests. They gave advice freely.

**Implication:** This industry has great people. If I'm smart and kind, I can build real professional relationships.

## The Meta-Learning: Why Networking > Grinding

### The Economic Argument

**Grinding the CTF:**
- 8 hours → maybe +2-3 flags → maybe +20-30 points → maybe move from 50th to 45th place

**Networking:**
- 5 hours → 1 job offer + 4 professional contacts → potential career acceleration

In pure ROI, networking won.

### The Personal Argument

I spent the day before RVASEC grinding on my laptop. I'll spend the next 100 days doing the same (school, CTF practice, personal projects). 

Two days at a conference is *rare*. Two days on a laptop is common.

Should optimize for what's rare.

### The Skill-Building Argument

I can improve technical skills on my own. I can't improve networking skills without... networking.

RVASEC was optimal leverage for building soft skills.

## Lessons for Next Competition/Conference

### 1. Know Your Goal

- **Internal club competition:** Maximize learning (done at JMU mock)
- **External CTF competition:** Maximize technical ranking (MACCDC, Summit)
- **Conference:** Maximize relationships & industry insight

Different goals → different strategies.

### 2. Prepare Differently

**For CTF competitions:** Study technical challenges, bring laptop ready

**For conferences:** Prepare a 30-second introduction, research attendees beforehand, bring business cards

### 3. Balance Matters

At a conference, don't neglect the competition entirely (looks bad, wastes opportunity). But don't grind so hard you miss why you're there.

I solved 8 challenges = showed competence. Then attended talks = showed good judgment.

## My Changed Perspective on Cybersecurity Career

### Before RVASEC

"Get really good at technical skills. Win competitions. That's how you stand out."

### After RVASEC

"Get good at technical skills AND develop your ability to communicate, listen, and build relationships. Win competitions if you enjoy them, but prioritize meeting smart people doing interesting work."

### The New Plan

**Technical Development (Same):**
- Specialize in Windows defense + privilege escalation
- Continue CTF competitions (enjoyment + learning)
- Deep-dive into 2-3 specific domains

**Professional Development (New):**
- Attend conferences (RVASEC next year for sure)
- Write about what I learn (blog, writeups, talks)
- Build genuine relationships with people in the industry
- Practice explaining technical things to non-technical people
- Consider government contracting path (clearance interesting)

**The Realization:** I don't need to choose between being "technical" and being "professional." I can be both. The market actually *rewards* people who are technical *and* can communicate.

## Conclusion: RVASEC15 Was Really About This

I went to RVASEC15 planning to "prove myself" by winning a CTF competition.

I ended up learning something more valuable: I can't prove myself to an industry. I can only *join* an industry by being willing to listen, learn, and build relationships with people who are further along.

The CTF challenges will always be there. The people at RVASEC will be there next year, but they might remember me next time if I reach out first. That's worth more than any trophy.

Also: I can't wait for next year.

RVASEC taught me that.
