# RVASEC15

[← All write-ups](../README.md)

Went planning to compete in the CTF. Left with something more useful.

Jake Kouns — security industry veteran, knew our club advisor — gave JMU tickets directly. That turned out to matter more than I expected going in. Walking in with an implicit voucher from someone people recognize meant professionals were actually willing to talk.

Day 1, I went to the first keynote just to orient myself and never came back to the CTF. A panel of CISOs and founders discussing the future of the field, and one of them said something like: "The best security person isn't the one who knows the most tools. It's the one who can communicate what they know to non-technical stakeholders." I closed my laptop.

Spent the rest of Day 1 in conversations. Threat intel analyst at a Fortune 500, security startup founder, a red team operator. Every single one came back to the same thing: technical skill is the entry ticket, communication is what differentiates you. Can you write the report? Can you explain this to a non-technical CEO? Do people want to work with you? The red teamer specifically: "Every red teamer can escalate privileges. What separates you is whether you can document it clearly and explain to a client why it matters."

Day 2 I solved 8 challenges in an hour (easy-medium range), hit something hard, gave up after 15 minutes, and went to talks instead. Booth crawl in the afternoon. Left with 4-5 emails from people who explicitly said reach out after graduation.

Wouldn't have gotten any of that grinding a laptop for 8 hours.

## The shift

Competitions train technical skills. Conferences train everything else. CTF practice is always available — it's online, unlimited, I can do it any day. I can attend RVASEC once a year. Optimize for what's rare.

Also: being good and people knowing you're good are different things. Planning to write more, document more, build something that exists outside of competition scoreboards.
